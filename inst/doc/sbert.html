Educational software now writes much of the feedback that learners read. An application that generates its hints, corrections and explanations with a language model produces thousands of messages, many of them near copies of one another, and the question an evaluator asks of such a corpus is what kinds of feedback it contains and how often each kind is given. Counting words does not answer that question. Frequent words and phrases recover the templates the generator reuses (“choose all the pictures”, “in the red box”), not what a message does for the learner: whether it hints, asks, corrects or explains.

Semantic topic modeling groups texts by meaning instead of by shared words. Each text is encoded as a sentence embedding, a vector in which texts with similar meaning lie close together even when they share few words (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). The embeddings are normalized to unit length and partitioned by k-means (Lloyd, 1982), so that a topic is a region of the embedding space. A topic is then described in two ways: by the texts nearest its center, which show what its members say, and by the words that are frequent in it and rare elsewhere, scored with class-based TF-IDF (Grootendorst, 2022). The sbert package computes the embeddings in R, without Python, and fits the topics deterministically, so the same input always gives the same topics, tables and figures.

Encoding needs a model that is downloaded once on request. This article does not download one: the embeddings of the messages it models are bundled with the package, and every analysis below runs on them. The step that would compute them is shown, not run.

library(sbert)

Data

feedback_translations holds 8,757 AI-generated feedback messages from a mathematics learning application, written in ten source languages and translated into English.

  • feedback: the message in its source language. Carried along with each modeled message, not modeled.
  • translation: the English translation. The text that is modeled.
  • n: added by dedupe(), the number of times the message occurs in the full corpus. Used to weight topic sizes.

Many messages repeat word for word. dedupe() keeps the first occurrence of each distinct translation, with all its columns, and counts its occurrences in n:

messages <- dedupe(feedback_translations, column = "translation")
nrow(messages)

subset(messages, n >= 7, select = c(translation, n))
#>                                                                translation  n
#> 127                                                             Try again. 11
#> 305 How many pictures were there? Now you see three. How many are missing?  7
#> 712                                                     The first picture.  7
#> 790                                             There must be another way.  7
#> 874   How many pictures were there? Now you see one. How many are missing?  7

The corpus has 8,005 distinct translations. The most repeated message, “Try again.”, occurs 11 times, and four more messages occur 7 times each. Repetition is therefore moderate, but it is uneven, which matters when topic sizes are reported.

The analysis uses the first 600 distinct messages, whose embeddings ship with the package as feedback_embeddings: row i of that matrix is the embedding of row i of corpus.

corpus <- head(messages, 600)
dim(feedback_embeddings)
#> [1] 600 384
with(corpus, sum(n))
#> [1] 740

Each of the 600 messages is a vector of 384 numbers from the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model, and together they were shown 740 times. With a downloaded model, the same analysis starts from the text alone, and topics() encodes it; this chunk is not run here:

model_download()
topic_model <- topics(corpus, n_topics = 6, column = "translation")

How many kinds of feedback are there?

There is no correct number of topics. compare_topics() fits one model for each candidate count and reports, for each:

  • coherence: whether each topic’s top words occur together in the same messages, as normalized pointwise mutual information (NPMI; Bouma, 2009), from -1 to 1, averaged over topics.
  • topic_diversity: the share of distinct words among all topics’ top-10 word lists; 1 when no two topics share a top word.
  • explained: the share of the variation in the embeddings that the topics capture. It grows with every added topic, so it cannot choose the count on its own.
  • converged: whether k-means reached a stable partition.

Coherence and diversity are computed from words, so they are a guide; the test of a count is whether the messages of each topic do the same thing, which the following sections check.

sweep <- compare_topics(corpus, column = "translation",
                        n_topics = c(4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12),
                        embeddings = feedback_embeddings)
sweep

Every candidate converged. Coherence is negative at every count, because the messages are short and two content words rarely occur in the same message, and it is highest at six topics (-0.104), with eight close behind (-0.118). What are the topics?

topic_sizes() lists the topics. The label of each topic is the message closest to its center, cut at 60 characters, and n_documents counts its messages:

topic_sizes(topic_model)
#>   topic                                                       label n_documents
#> 1     1   Where is the cow and the cake? Choose all the pictures...         223
#> 2     2            All the ones between the cat and the strawberry.         133
#> 3     3         What does “all the ones between the leaf and the...         131
#> 4     4 Are you sure you have fewer pictures in the red box than...          49
#> 5     5      In the top middle box, it means something is in the...          37
#> 6     6    It is not a person, does not have a hat, and is sitting.          27
#>   proportion
#> 1     0.3717
#> 2     0.2217
#> 3     0.2183
#> 4     0.0817
#> 5     0.0617
#> 6     0.0450

Reading the labels and the messages of each topic (shown in the next section) shows that the topics are not subjects but kinds of feedback, defined by what a message does for the learner:

  1. Guiding question and instruction. A question that makes the learner find something, followed by an order to act on it: Where is the cow and the cake? Choose all the pictures that are between them.
  2. Short hint. A fragment with no verb that restates the target: All the ones between the cat and the strawberry.
  3. Question about meaning. A question that asks the learner what a phrase means: What does “all the ones between the leaf and the unicorn” mean?
  4. Correction. A statement of what should be true, or a question that makes the learner check an answer: Are you sure you have fewer pictures in the red box than in the blue one?
  5. Definition. An explanation of what a word or phrase means: In the top middle box, it means something is in the middle at the top.
  6. Negation. A description built from what something is not, used to rule out answers: It is not a person, does not have a hat, and is sitting.

Topics 1, 2 and 3 concern the same exercise, finding the pictures between two others, and use the same words. The embeddings still separate them, because an instruction, a hint and a question do different things. Topics 3 and 5 both deal with meaning, but one asks the learner and the other tells them.

name_topics() gives the topics these names, one per topic in topic order. The names replace the labels in every table and plot that follows; nothing is refitted.

topic_model <- name_topics(topic_model, c(
  "Guiding question and instruction", "Short hint",
  "Question about meaning", "Correction", "Definition", "Negation"
))
topic_sizes(topic_model)
#>   topic                            label n_documents proportion
#> 1     1 Guiding question and instruction         223     0.3717
#> 2     2                       Short hint         133     0.2217
#> 3     3           Question about meaning         131     0.2183
#> 4     4                       Correction          49     0.0817
#> 5     5                       Definition          37     0.0617
#> 6     6                         Negation          27     0.0450

Instructions are the largest kind of feedback, with 223 of the 600 distinct messages (37.2%), and negations the smallest, with 27 (4.5%).

How typical are the example messages?

representatives() returns the messages of each topic ranked by how typical they are. With rank = "distance" the ranking is by the cosine distance from the message to its topic’s center; margin is how much farther the message is from the next closest topic. The text column is long, so the table is printed with knitr::kable():

The most typical messages lie between 0.14 and 0.33 from their centers. Corrections and negations are the tightest topics: their nearest messages are within 0.17 of the center, and their margins (0.40 to 0.50) are the largest in the table, so these messages could belong to no other kind of feedback. Definitions are the loosest: their closest message is 0.29 from the center, and the topic mixes explanations of a phrase with questions about it, the boundary it shares with topic 3. The default, rank = "margin", ranks by margin instead and favors messages that are unambiguous members of their topic.

The plot shows the same messages, with the similarity to the center in parentheses:

plot(topic_model, type = "representatives", n_representatives = 3)

How large are the topics once repetition is counted?

n_documents counts distinct messages, so a message shown four times counts once. Passing the name of the count column as weights adds n_weighted and weighted_share, which count every time a message was shown:

topic_sizes(topic_model, weights = "n")
#>   topic                            label n_documents proportion n_weighted weighted_share
#> 1     1 Guiding question and instruction         223     0.3717        267         0.3608
#> 2     2                       Short hint         133     0.2217        162         0.2189
#> 3     3           Question about meaning         131     0.2183        138         0.1865
#> 4     4                       Correction          49     0.0817         76         0.1027
#> 5     5                       Definition          37     0.0617         57         0.0770
#> 6     6                         Negation          27     0.0450         40         0.0541

Corrections grow from 8.2% of the distinct messages to 10.3% of all messages shown, and questions about meaning shrink from 21.8% to 18.6%. The application repeated its corrections more often than its other kinds of feedback, so a count of distinct messages understates how much correcting it did.

plot(topic_model, type = "sizes")

What words does each topic use?

Words do not define these topics; the sentences do. The words a topic uses more than the others still help to search the messages or to see how a topic is phrased. terms() lists them; the score is class-based TF-IDF, high for words frequent in this topic and rare in the others:

The words show why they make poor names. They describe what the messages are about, not what they do. The top words of the short hints, after ones, are names of pictures (broccoli, candy, hippo) that change from message to message, and nothing in them says that the topic is made of hints. The words of the negations (person, animal, hat) name the pictures, not the negation that defines the topic. Short hints and questions about meaning share the word ones, although one gives a hint and the other asks a question.

The whole model in one figure

type = "fit" shows each topic’s example messages and, beside them, its words, one row per topic:

plot(topic_model, type = "fit", n_terms = 4, n_representatives = 3)

The figure is the report of the analysis: six kinds of feedback, each with the messages that define it and the vocabulary that phrases it.

When to use which

Question Verb
How many topics, at which granularity? compare_topics(), summary(), fitted()
What are the topics and how large are they? topic_sizes(), topic_sizes(weights = )
What do the topics say? representatives(), name_topics()
Which words phrase them? terms(), coherence(), topic_diversity()
Which topic does a new message belong to? predict(), topic_membership()
What mix of topics is in a long document? topics(segment = "sentence"), topic_gamma()
